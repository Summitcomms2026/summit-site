## Editorial Philosophy

Summit’s editorial system exists to do four things at once:

1. build category authority in global luxury
2. support the consultancy without behaving like content marketing wallpaper
3. create owned audience value over time
4. lay the groundwork for a valuable media enterprise

The editorial system should feel:
- thoughtful
- selective
- internationally relevant
- culturally literate
- commercially serious
- editorial rather than promotional
- British in tone, global in relevance

Summit should not publish like:
- a content farm
- an agency trying to look busy
- a newsletter guru with a ring light
- a luxury moodboard masquerading as analysis

The standard is simple:
every published item should either sharpen Summit’s authority, deepen audience relationship or strengthen the media ecosystem.

## Editorial Architecture

Summit’s editorial system has two primary public expressions.

### 1. The Future of Luxury
This is Summit’s flagship editorial property.

It exists to publish:
- analysis
- commentary
- interviews
- strategic essays
- category intelligence
- long-form thought leadership

Its role is to establish Summit as a serious voice on:
- luxury strategy
- culture
- technology
- affluent audiences
- value, status and experience
- the future of premium categories

### 2. Tastemakers
This is Summit’s media property.

It exists to publish:
- podcast series
- episode pages
- transcripts
- guest-led insights
- supporting editorial extensions
- season-led thematic storytelling

Its role is to deepen Summit’s authority through conversation, voice, network and recurring audience engagement.

### Relationship Between Them
- The Future of Luxury is the essayistic and analytical layer.
- Tastemakers is the conversational and media layer.
- Both should support Summit’s consultancy authority.
- Neither should read like a hidden sales brochure in a tailored coat.

## Editorial Formats

Summit should publish in a controlled set of formats.

### The Future of Luxury Formats

#### 1. Long-Form Essay
Purpose:
- category authority
- search visibility
- deep thought leadership

Typical characteristics:
- 1,200–3,000 words
- argument-led
- subheaded
- internally linkable
- citation-aware where needed

#### 2. Commentary
Purpose:
- quick strategic response
- topical analysis
- point of view

Typical characteristics:
- 700–1,400 words
- sharper
- more immediate
- opinion-led but grounded

#### 3. Interview / Q&A
Purpose:
- borrow authority intelligently
- deepen category texture
- build network value

Typical characteristics:
- editorial introduction
- properly structured Q&A or essay-with-quotes
- strategic framing, not transcript dump

#### 4. Briefing / Explainer
Purpose:
- clarify a concept
- support service relevance
- strengthen search authority

Typical characteristics:
- structured
- precise
- useful
- less essayistic, more explanatory

#### 5. Visual Essay / Download-Led Piece
Purpose:
- support premium assets
- bridge design and ideas
- create gated or ungated authority objects

Typical characteristics:
- concise intro
- strong visual framing
- ties to downloadable deck or document

### Tastemakers Formats

#### 1. Series Landing
Purpose:
- define the proposition
- position the show
- route users into seasons and episodes

#### 2. Season Page
Purpose:
- establish a season thesis
- organise episodes into a coherent argument
- create a more editorial experience than a list of uploads

#### 3. Episode Page
Purpose:
- hold the media object
- host the transcript
- capture search traffic
- deepen the conversation around the guest/topic

#### 4. Supporting Editorial Extension
Purpose:
- connect a strong episode to a larger Summit argument
- extend the life of interviews and conversations

## Publishing Cadence

Cadence should be realistic, premium and sustainable.

### The Future of Luxury
Recommended starting cadence:
- 1 major article per week
- plus optional 1 shorter commentary or briefing where worthwhile

This is enough to build momentum without diluting standards.

### Tastemakers
Recommended starting cadence:
- publish by season
- maintain a consistent episode rhythm during active season
- use off-cycle periods for:
  - transcript publication
  - related articles
  - clips
  - season summaries
  - guest-led extensions

### Newsletter / Audience Touchpoints
Recommended:
- 1 regular editorial dispatch per week
- optional special sends for:
  - major articles
  - season launches
  - premium downloads
  - webinars
  - notable guest episodes

### Rule
Consistency matters more than volume.  
Summit should feel deliberate, not content-hungry.

## Editorial Workflow

### Stage 1 — Idea / Commission
An item begins as one of the following:
- strategic idea
- response to a market development
- category pattern worth exploring
- podcast episode derived from guest or season thesis
- extension of client-facing insight worth turning into public authority
- event / webinar / interview output worth converting into editorial form

### Stage 2 — Triage
Classify as:
- article
- commentary
- briefing
- interview
- episode
- season page update
- download-led piece
- internal only / not for publication

### Stage 3 — Draft
Create the first working draft.

### Stage 4 — Editorial Review
Check for:
- relevance
- clarity
- argument strength
- Summit voice
- structure
- overstatement
- usefulness
- strategic fit

### Stage 5 — Structural Preparation
Add:
- taxonomy
- metadata
- internal links
- related content
- CTA logic
- imagery / artwork
- transcript formatting if relevant

### Stage 6 — Publish
Only when the item is:
- structurally sound
- on-brand
- strategically useful
- technically clean

### Stage 7 — Distribution
Push into:
- newsletter
- Buffer / social scheduling
- LinkedIn
- Tastemakers promotion
- internal linking and follow-on pieces

### Stage 8 — Review
Assess:
- reach
- engagement
- audience capture
- strategic value
- whether follow-up content is warranted

### Primary Measures
- article engagement
- repeat visits
- newsletter sign-ups
- episode engagement
- time on page / reading quality
- conversion into enquiry or known audience where relevant
- topic cluster performance over time

### Secondary Measures
- social reach
- clip performance
- traffic spikes
- referral patterns
- season-level listener response

### Real Strategic Measures

- did it deepen the archive?
- did it generate the right kind of attention?
- did it create useful audience relationships?
- did it support business development without cheapening the editorial brand?

### Rule
One intelligent reader who matters is often worth more than one thousand meaningless impressions.

## Content Quality Rules

Every published item should meet the following tests:

1. Is it genuinely useful or illuminating?
2. Is the argument or premise strong enough?
3. Is it on-brand for Summit?
4. Is the structure clean?
5. Is the tone British, composed and globally legible?
6. Does it add something to the archive?
7. Is there a reason this exists on Summit rather than as a fleeting post elsewhere?
8. Is the metadata, linking and publishing hygiene complete?

If the answer to several of these is no, do not publish it yet.

---

## Editorial Anti-Patterns

Avoid:
- publishing for the sake of activity
- agency-style filler articles
- trend-jumping without substance
- badly edited transcript dumps
- commentary without a point of view
- newsletters that read like software templates
- overproduction without strategic value
- under-editing because a platform generated the first draft
- calling everything thought leadership until the phrase dies entirely

If the archive grows in volume faster than it grows in quality, the system is failing.

## Editorial Success Criteria

The editorial system is successful when:
- The Future of Luxury feels like a serious owned publication
- Tastemakers feels like a real media property, not a side project
- articles and episodes link intelligently into the wider Summit ecosystem
- the newsletter becomes an owned audience asset
- the archive gains authority over time
- publishing remains consistent without becoming industrial
- the consultancy benefits from trust rather than sales camouflage
- Summit becomes harder to ignore in the category it has chosen

In short:  
The editorial system is successful when Summit begins to feel less like a firm with content and more like an intelligent house with a point of view.
