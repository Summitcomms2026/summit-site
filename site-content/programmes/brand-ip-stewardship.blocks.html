Not Everything Can Be Protected by Law

A house may own a mark and still lose the meaning it once carried. Equally, a skill or ritual may have enormous institutional value without fitting neatly inside a registered right.

A serious stewardship plan knows the difference. Law, documentation, creative continuity, commercial discipline and human expertise each have a part to play.

Good Questions

Is this the same as a legal IP audit?

No. Stewardship looks at the broader system around a maison’s assets: what carries identity, how it is documented, whether the organisation still controls its narrative and where commercial decisions create exposure. Formal legal opinions, filings or litigation require appropriately qualified legal advisers.

Can forgotten archives create new commercial value?

They can inform design, product, provenance and editorial work when the evidence and rights permit it. But an archive does not become useful simply by being displayed. The test is whether historical knowledge is brought into the living practices of the house without misrepresenting it.

When should a growing brand begin protecting its IP?

As early as the material assets and distinctive proposition can be identified. The New Asian Maison argues that fame is often when others learn what is worth copying. The precise timing and form of protection depend on jurisdiction, ownership, rights availability and professional legal assessment.

Can the programme help during an acquisition or succession?

It can help frame questions about control, provenance, key-person knowledge, archive continuity and institutional exposure. That reading should complement, not replace, formal legal, financial, tax or transaction diligence. Sensitive material belongs within an agreed secure process, not a public enquiry form.
